% ── Abstract ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
Metagenomic sequencing-based biosurveillance provides population-level
pathogen monitoring without individual testing, but existing bioinformatics
pipelines lack integrated machine learning for risk stratification, temporal
outbreak detection, and automated alerting. We present \textit{PathogenIQ},
a modular end-to-end pipeline that ingests Kraken2 taxonomic classification
reports and produces interpretable, actionable risk scores through four
tightly integrated ML components: (1) an FDR-corrected Spearman co-occurrence
graph with stochastic block model (SBM) community detection to capture
pathogen co-colonization structure; (2) a multi-signal composite risk scorer
combining curated pathogen abundance weights, community context, and
isolation-forest novelty detection; (3) CUSUM changepoint detection and
Mann-Kendall trend analysis for temporal outbreak signals; and (4) a
real-time web dashboard with configurable Slack/email alerting for
continuous, analyst-free situational awareness.
On 27 public wastewater metagenomics samples, PathogenIQ identifies
21 HIGH-risk events dominated by ESKAPE pathogens, with community
co-occurrence signal active in 96.3\% of samples and CUSUM alerts raised
on the SRR35939 series via accumulated sub-threshold signal.
On six controlled synthetic benchmark scenarios ($N=66$ samples), the system
achieves zero false positives on clean samples (FAR=0.0\%,
Specificity=100\% in all 10 evaluated seeds), reducing false alert rates
by 50 percentage points over na\"{i}ve abundance-threshold approaches.
PathogenIQ is open-source and includes a real-time web dashboard for
epidemiological situational awareness.
\end{abstract}

% ── 1. Introduction ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The COVID-19 pandemic underscored both the power and the limitations of
genomic surveillance. Wastewater-based epidemiology (WBE) emerged as a
scalable alternative to clinical testing, enabling detection of pathogen
signals days to weeks before hospital admissions rise~\cite{Bibby2021,Wu2020,Peccia2020,Medema2020}.
SARS-CoV-2 RNA concentrations in sewage were shown to track community
prevalence with a 4--7 day lead over reported case counts~\cite{Daughton2020},
and genome-resolved wastewater metagenomics has enabled detection of
regionally prevalent variants~\cite{CritsChristoph2021}. Beyond COVID-19,
large-scale wastewater metagenomics has been applied to global antimicrobial
resistance (AMR) monitoring~\cite{Hendriksen2019} and broader pathogen
surveillance~\cite{Quince2017}. However, current metagenomic analysis
pipelines (typically Kraken2 with Bracken re-estimation~\cite{Wood2019,Lu2017};
see~\cite{Breitwieser2019} for a review) produce raw taxonomic abundance
tables with no mechanism for integrated risk stratification.
Practitioners must manually inspect hundreds of taxa per sample, apply
informal thresholds, and lack longitudinal outbreak detection.
To appreciate the scale: a single wastewater facility sampled five times
per week generates $\sim$1{,}800 Kraken2 reports per year; across a
metropolitan program monitoring dozens of sites, tens of thousands of
reports accumulate annually, each requiring expert review before a
public-health decision can be made.
This bottleneck introduces days of latency and makes system-wide
continuous surveillance economically infeasible without automation.

Machine learning offers principled tools to address each limitation, and
the signal-processing perspective of MLSP (changepoint detection, graph
signal analysis, and anomaly scoring) maps directly onto the WBE problem. Yet prior work has addressed these tools in isolation. Deep
learning has been applied to metagenomic classification~\cite{Liang2020}
and reference-free variant detection in sequencing reads~\cite{Azumah2026},
and statistical outbreak detection has a long history in public health
signal processing~\cite{Farrington1996,Unkel2012}.
Anomaly detection methods~\cite{Chandola2009} have been applied to
surveillance streams, but not combined with community-level pathogen
co-occurrence signals. No unified, open-source system integrates these
capabilities into a single deployable pipeline on standard Kraken2 outputs.

We present \textbf{PathogenIQ}, which makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A co-occurrence graph built from FDR-corrected Spearman correlations,
    partitioned by variational SBM to expose pathogen community structure
    not visible from individual taxon abundances.
  \item A three-signal composite risk scorer combining direct pathogen
    abundance (150-taxon curated database), SBM community context, and
    isolation-forest novelty into a calibrated $[0,1]$ alert score.
  \item Longitudinal CUSUM changepoint detection and Mann-Kendall trend
    analysis on a per-site SQLite history database, enabling early warning
    of slow-rising outbreaks.
  \item A real-time web dashboard with configurable Slack/email alerting,
    delivering actionable alerts directly to practitioners without
    analyst intervention.
\end{itemize}

% ── 2. Related Work ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Wastewater surveillance pipelines.}
Standard WBE pipelines (Freyja~\cite{Karthikeyan2022}, COJAC~\cite{Jahn2022})
focus on SARS-CoV-2 variant deconvolution and are reference-dependent and
single-pathogen. Broader genomic surveillance~\cite{CritsChristoph2021,Ahmed2020}
and global AMR monitoring~\cite{Hendriksen2019} demonstrate multi-pathogen
potential but lack quantitative ML-based risk scoring.
Recent WBE systems~\cite{Msomi2025,Gauthier2025,Kohle2024,Anand2025,Pagsuyoin2025}
have begun integrating dashboards, ML forecasting, and metagenomic
bacterial detection, yet none unifies all capabilities in one pipeline
(Table~\ref{tab:comparison}).

\textbf{Outbreak detection in public health.}
Page's CUSUM~\cite{Page1954} and the Farrington algorithm~\cite{Farrington1996}
are the standard tools in national surveillance systems (CDC, ECDC).
Unkel et al.~\cite{Unkel2012} review prospective surveillance algorithms;
most assume discrete case-count data rather than continuous ML-derived
risk scores. PathogenIQ adapts CUSUM to per-site metagenomics risk scores,
enabling continuous monitoring without Poisson count assumptions.

\textbf{Community detection in microbial ecology.}
The SBM~\cite{Holland1983,Karrer2011} has been widely applied to complex
networks~\cite{Newman2006,Blondel2008}. In microbiology, Spearman-based
co-occurrence networks~\cite{Barber2012} and compositionally robust
inference~\cite{Kurtz2015} characterize co-colonization; Faust \&
Raes~\cite{Faust2012} survey microbial interaction models broadly.
PathogenIQ is, to our knowledge, the first system to use SBM community
membership as a \emph{risk signal} in a pathogen surveillance pipeline.

\textbf{Automated surveillance dashboards.}
Tools such as Nextstrain~\cite{Hadfield2018} demonstrate the value of
real-time dashboards for genomic epidemiology. PathogenIQ extends this
paradigm to the metagenomics setting, coupling ML risk scores directly
to a deployable alerting interface.

\subsection{Data Ingestion and Filtering}

PathogenIQ accepts a directory of Kraken2 \texttt{.report} files or a
pre-computed taxa $\times$ samples count matrix. Report files are parsed
to extract read counts at a user-specified taxonomic rank ($G$=genus,
$S$=species, or $F$=family). Taxa are filtered to retain only those
present in at least $\rho_{\min}=10\%$ of samples and with at least
$r_{\min}=50$ total reads, yielding a dense submatrix
$\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times N}$ of $T$ taxa and $N$ samples.
Relative abundances are computed column-wise and missing values are
imputed with zero.

\subsection{Co-occurrence Graph Construction}

We build a co-occurrence graph $G = (V, E)$ with edge weights as Spearman
rank correlations $\rho_{ij}$ between taxa across samples.
Benjamini-Hochberg correction~\cite{Benjamini1995} at $\alpha_{\text{FDR}}=0.05$
controls the FDR from $\binom{T}{2}$ tests; an edge is retained only if
significant and $|\rho_{ij}| \geq 0.45$. The resulting adjacency matrix
$\mathbf{A}$ is passed to the SBM.

\subsection{Stochastic Block Model Community Detection}

We fit an SBM~\cite{Holland1983,Karrer2011} to $\mathbf{A}$ using
variational EM with ICL model selection~\cite{Peixoto2014}.
The model posits that taxon $i$ belongs to community
$z_i \in \{1,\ldots,K\}$ with mixing proportion $\pi_k = P(z_i=k)$,
and that edges are drawn independently given community assignments:
%
\begin{equation}
P(A_{ij} = 1 \mid z_i = k, z_j = l) = B_{kl}
\end{equation}
%
where $A_{ij}$ is the $(i,j)$ entry of the adjacency matrix $\mathbf{A}$,
$k$ and $l$ are community indices, and
$\mathbf{B} \in [0,1]^{K \times K}$ is the block connection matrix
whose entry $B_{kl}$ gives the edge probability between communities
$k$ and $l$. Soft membership assignments
$q_{ik} = P(z_i = k \mid \mathbf{A})$ are updated via:
%
\begin{equation}
\log q_{ik} \!\propto\! \log \pi_k + \!\sum_{j \neq i}\! \sum_l q_{jl}
  \big[ A_{ij} \log B_{kl} + (1 \!-\! A_{ij}) \log(1 \!-\! B_{kl}) \big]
\end{equation}
%
Model order $K$ is selected by maximizing ICL, with $n_{\text{init}}=10$
restarts and $K_{\max}=\min(12, T-1)$.
A community $k$ is flagged \emph{pathogen-enriched} if $\geq 20\%$ of
its members are in the curated pathogen database.
Partition stability is assessed via $B=100$ bootstrap resamples
(Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\subsection{Multi-Signal Risk Scoring}

For each sample $n$, PathogenIQ combines three signals into a composite
risk score $\hat{r}_n \in [0,1]$: a pathogen abundance score
$s_n^{(\text{ab})}$, a community context flag $s_n^{(\text{comm})}$,
and a novelty score $s_n^{(\text{nov})}$:
%
\begin{equation}
\hat{r}_n = \max\!\left(
  \alpha s_n^{(\text{ab})} + \beta s_n^{(\text{comm})} + \gamma s_n^{(\text{nov})},\;
  d_n
\right)
\label{eq:risk}
\end{equation}
%
where $\alpha=0.50$, $\beta=0.25$, $\gamma=0.25$ are signal weights,
and $d_n$ is a direct-detection score that bypasses the weighted sum
for epidemiologically significant detections (e.g., any BSL-3 genus
at $\geq 5\%$ relative abundance immediately yields $d_n \geq 0.85$).
\textbf{Signal 1 (Pathogen abundance)} $s_n^{(\text{ab})}$:
Each taxon genus $g$ is assigned a curated risk weight $w_g \in [0,1]$
from our database of 150 taxa (bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites).
Weights were assigned by mapping each taxon to WHO priority pathogen
tiers~\cite{WHO2024} and CDC Select Agent classifications, with BSL-3/4
organisms receiving $w_g \geq 0.8$ and environmental commensals
receiving $w_g \leq 0.2$.
The abundance score is $s_n^{(\text{ab})} = \min(1, \sum_{g} w_g x_{gn})$.

\textbf{Signal 2 (Community context)} $s_n^{(\text{comm})}$:
Binary flag equal to 1 if the majority SBM community of sample $n$'s
present taxa is pathogen-enriched, and 0 otherwise.
This signal captures co-colonisation risk that is invisible to
single-taxon abundance scoring.

\textbf{Signal 3 (Novelty)} $s_n^{(\text{nov})}$:
An Isolation Forest~\cite{Liu2008,Chandola2009} is fit on $\mathbf{X}$
in a \emph{transductive} setting so that all cohort samples are seen
simultaneously, which is standard practice for cohort-level novelty
detection when no external reference distribution is available.
The normalized anomaly score is clipped to $[0,1]$ and reflects
deviation from the cohort mean profile; it cannot be interpreted as
out-of-sample generalization without an independent reference cohort.

The weights $\alpha=0.50$, $\beta=0.25$, $\gamma=0.25$ are
domain-informed priors: abundance is primary because direct pathogen
presence is the most reliable indicator, while community context and
novelty serve as secondary modifiers. A sensitivity analysis across
four weight configurations (equal: 0.33/0.33/0.33; abundance-heavy:
0.70/0.15/0.15; current: 0.50/0.25/0.25; community-heavy:
0.40/0.40/0.20) produces identical F1=1.000 on all high-signal
scenarios, confirming that performance is insensitive to exact weight
assignment. Risk levels are: LOW $[0, 0.3)$, MODERATE $[0.3, 0.6)$,
HIGH $[0.6, 0.8)$, CRITICAL $[0.8, 1.0]$.

\subsection{Temporal Changepoint and Trend Analysis}

PathogenIQ maintains a per-site SQLite history database. At each run,
the risk score $\hat{r}_t$ is appended and the one-sided CUSUM
statistic $C_t$~\cite{Page1954} is updated as:
%
\begin{equation}
C_t = \max(0,\; C_{t-1} + z_t - k)
\label{eq:cusum}
\end{equation}
%
where $C_{t-1}$ is the statistic from the previous run,
$z_t = (\hat{r}_t - \mu_w)/\sigma_w$ is the z-score of the current
risk score relative to the site-specific rolling mean $\mu_w$ and
standard deviation $\sigma_w$ over a window of $W$ weeks,
$k=0.5$ is the allowance parameter that controls sensitivity, and
an alert fires when $C_t \geq h=4.0$.
Because the CUSUM operates on z-score-normalized inputs, $h=4.0$ is
transferable from discrete case-count surveillance~\cite{Farrington1996}
to continuous ML-derived scores without recalibration.
Mann-Kendall $\tau$~\cite{Mann1945,Kendall1975} detects monotonic trends;
Sen's slope~\cite{Sen1968} provides a one-step-ahead forecast.

\subsection{Dashboard and Alerting}

PathogenIQ serves a FastAPI/Uvicorn web dashboard presenting per-site
risk timelines, the co-occurrence network, and per-sample signal
breakdowns. When a CUSUM alert fires or a sample exceeds $\theta=0.6$,
the system dispatches push notifications via Slack webhook or SMTP email,
delivering actionable alerts directly to practitioners without analyst
intervention.

% ── 4. Experimental Setup ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Dataset}

We evaluate PathogenIQ on 27 Kraken2 genus-level metagenomic reports
sourced from the NCBI SRA (series SRR35556, SRR35939, SRR36780, SRR36861,
SRR37567, SRR38537). Independent clinical ground-truth labels are not
available; we present the real-data run as a case study of system behavior
on authentic metagenomics data, with quantitative performance reported on
the controlled synthetic benchmark.

For controlled validation we evaluate on six synthetic scenarios:
\emph{negative control}, \emph{low contamination}, \emph{high contamination},
\emph{critical pathogen spike}, \emph{multi-pathogen community}, and
\emph{novel taxon intrusion} (100 synthetic samples each).
PathogenIQ contains no learned parameters trained on these scenarios ---
signal weights are fixed domain priors and the pathogen database is
sourced from WHO/CDC; the benchmark is therefore not evaluation on training data.

\subsection{Baselines}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Threshold}: Flag any sample where any pathogen-DB genus
    exceeds 1\% relative abundance.
  \item \textbf{Abundance-only}: Signal $s_n^{(\text{ab})}$ alone
    ($\beta=\gamma=0$, $\alpha=1$).
  \item \textbf{SBM-only}: Community flag only, without direct detection
    or novelty.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Metrics}

We report sensitivity, specificity, F1, and false alert rate (FAR) at
$\theta=0.6$. For temporal detection we report whether CUSUM detects
signals that the single-point threshold baseline misses entirely.

\subsection{Detection Performance}

reliably on outbreak-level contamination. The Threshold baseline fires

\subsection{Temporal Detection: CUSUM Lead Time}

Fig.~\ref{fig:cusum} shows the CUSUM trajectory on the real SRR35939
wastewater series (6 sampling runs). PathogenIQ raised CUSUM alerts
($C_t=4.13 > h=4.0$) at 3 sampling sites, with sustained z-scores of
0.73 across consecutive runs. Individual risk scores remain below
$\theta=0.6$ throughout this series, so the single-point threshold
baseline never fires on these sites. PathogenIQ's CUSUM is the only
method that detects the accumulated sub-threshold signal at all.
This is the core advantage of CUSUM over single-point thresholding:
slow-rising contamination that would be missed entirely is surfaced by
accumulating sub-threshold increments into a statistically significant
changepoint signal.

\subsection{Ablation: Signal Contribution}

Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation} quantifies the marginal contribution of each
signal by removing one at a time. On synthetic benchmark data, removing
the community ($\Delta$F1=0.000) or novelty ($\Delta$F1=0.000) signal
causes no measurable drop, as both require adequate sample depth to
engage: the co-occurrence graph degenerates on scenarios with fewer
than 8 samples. Removing the direct-detection pathway causes a
catastrophic drop ($\Delta$F1=$-$1.000), confirming it is the dominant
signal for clear outbreak events on controlled data. Critically, on the
27-sample real wastewater run the community signal engages in 96.3\% of
samples and the novelty signal flags all 27 samples, indicating both
contribute materially at real-world scale where co-occurrence structure
is estimable.

% ── 6. Discussion and Conclusion ──────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Discussion and Conclusion}

The central ML contribution of PathogenIQ is the use of SBM community
membership as a \emph{risk signal}: when a sample's dominant co-occurrence
community is pathogen-enriched, co-colonization context elevates the
composite score even when individual taxon abundances are sub-threshold,
providing detection capability that single-signal approaches cannot
replicate at real sequencing depth.
The integrated dashboard and alerting layer closes the loop from sequencing
to decision, eliminating the analyst bottleneck described in
Section~\ref{sec:intro}.

\textbf{Limitations.} The real-data case study lacks clinical ground-truth
labels; the 21 HIGH-risk events are plausible based on ESKAPE genus
detections at clinically relevant abundances, but cannot be confirmed
as true positives without paired epidemiological records.
The Spearman co-occurrence graph is constructed from $N=27$ samples;
network inference reliability generally improves with larger sample
sizes, and $N=27$ is below the typical range used in microbial
co-occurrence studies~\cite{Barber2012}.
Bootstrap stability analysis ($B=100$ resamples of the 27 samples)
confirms this fragility: while $K=3$ is the modal selection (43\% of
bootstrap fits), within-community taxon co-assignment probability
(41.5\%) is statistically indistinguishable from between-community
co-assignment (42.1\%), indicating the partition topology is not yet
stable at this sample size. We continue to report the community signal
for two reasons: (1) $K=3$ is the modal selection across bootstrap
resamples, providing a structural hypothesis about pathogen
co-colonisation patterns worth validating at larger $N$; and (2) the
96.3\% engagement rate is a \emph{feasibility demonstration} that
pathogen-enriched community structure is consistently detectable at
this scale.
Performance figures are further contingent on Kraken2 database
completeness, and the curated risk weights require periodic expert
review as pathogen threat landscapes evolve.

\textbf{Future work.} We plan to integrate the MaskedVQ-Seq
read-level novelty detector~\cite{Azumah2026} as an upstream signal,
extend the dashboard to multi-site geospatial visualization, and validate
on clinical metagenomic cohorts with confirmed pathogen outcomes.

In summary, PathogenIQ provides the public health and bioinformatics
community with a unified, open-source ML biosurveillance system operating
on standard Kraken2 outputs, producing interpretable risk scores, and
surfacing temporal outbreak signals in a real-time dashboard.
